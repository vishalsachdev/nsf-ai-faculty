Using AI to Learn AI

A hands-on teaching approach for the AI for Faculty initiative: helping faculty in every discipline understand how AI works, so they can use it responsibly in their research and guide the students who already do.

Faculty don't just learn about AI. They learn through AI, by directing an agent, watching it work, and stress-testing the results.

The AI for Faculty initiative began at the University of Illinois and is now a joint effort with colleagues at the University of Nebraska toward an NSF proposal. Its focus is faculty education: an interdisciplinary AI curriculum, delivered through institutes and short courses, built into research and graduate training, assessed carefully, and extended nationally.

This page sketches one way to teach that curriculum. The goal is not to turn faculty into AI developers. It is to give them enough understanding of how models learn, generalize and fail that they can evaluate AI-based research claims, mentor students, and use AI ethically in their own work.

Scenario 1: Supervising Research

Walkthrough example

A Faculty Mentor Checks a Student's 94% Accuracy

A social scientist's doctoral student reports that a machine-learning model predicts survey dropout with 94% accuracy. The advisor is not sure what questions to ask.

  1. Ask the agent: "Make a small fake dataset like my student's survey, train a simple model on it, and show me how accuracy is calculated."
  2. Watch it work: The agent writes and runs the code in a sandbox, then shows the data, the model and the score, step by step.
  3. Probe: "What if I test it on the same rows it trained on?" The score jumps. The advisor sees, with their own eyes, why a held-out test set matters.
  4. Stress-test: "Only 6% of people drop out. What does a model that always says 'stays' score?" About 94%. The headline number now has a baseline.
  5. Reflect: The advisor leaves with three questions for every AI-based result: what was it tested on, what is the baseline, and what would make it fail.
The learning moment

The advisor didn't read a slide about overfitting. They produced it and then caught it. That is the understanding a mentor needs to review a student's methods.

Scenario 2: Library and Research Integrity

Walkthrough example

A Librarian Discovers What AI Actually Sees

A reference librarian hears faculty claim that "AI can search all the literature." They want to know what is actually happening.

  1. Ask the agent: "Build a small script that shows exactly what a PubMed query for 'CRISPR gene therapy' returns. Show me the raw fields."
  2. Watch it work: The agent runs the query and shows titles, abstracts, MeSH terms and authors. No full text.
  3. Probe: "Where is the full text?" PubMed indexes metadata; full text often sits behind publisher paywalls.
  4. Stress-test: "So if a student says 'AI reviewed 500 papers,' what did it review?" The agent builds a side-by-side of what a human reads and what the tool accessed.
  5. Reflect: The librarian now has a concrete limitation to teach every student and faculty member they work with.

How It Works

Faculty work with an AI agent in a sandboxed environment. They don't need to code. They describe what they want to see, and the agent builds it live.

Each module pairs a short conceptual foundation with guided prompts for the faculty member's discipline, ideally using their own kind of data. Every exercise ends the same way: find where the AI is wrong, and explain why.

Fit With the Proposal

Proposal componentHow this approach supports it
Interdisciplinary AI curriculumA shared core on how models learn and fail, then discipline modules built from the same exercise pattern.
Institutes and short coursesExercises run in a 5 to 15 hour course or a multi-day institute. An in-person pilot cohort tests and refines them first.
Research-development environmentsFaculty practice on research-shaped data and questions, not generic chatbot demos.
Graduate educationAdvisors and their students can work through the same modules, giving them a common vocabulary for reviewing AI methods.
Assessing outcomesPre/post concept checks, plus the agent session logs, show what each participant tried, caught and missed.
National extension, starting with EPSCoRA train-the-trainer model: partner campuses send facilitators, then adapt the modules locally.
